Given your interests (typography, writing systems, Southeast Asian languages, etymology, philosophy, and using Fdf42 as a way to explore how information becomes visual form), I would not choose an endangered language only by number of speakers. I would choose one where the **language itself has something interesting to teach your project**.

Here are some endangered or vulnerable languages that could fit your interests.

---

## 1. Ainu language — a very strong match after Japanese

**Why it fits you:**

* It connects naturally with your planned Japanese learning.
* It represents a different linguistic history from Japanese despite geographic proximity.
* It has important cultural preservation work around it.

Interesting for Fdf42:

* Compare two languages from the same region but different origins.
* Explore how different phonologies and writing conventions affect visual representation.

Challenges:

* Very few fluent speakers remain.
* Learning resources are more limited than Japanese or Chinese.

---

## 2. Khmer language — especially relevant from Thailand

**Why it fits you:**

* You already have a connection to Southeast Asia.
* Khmer has one of the world's most complex writing systems.

Interesting for typography:

* Many consonant symbols.
* Subscripts ("sub-consonants").
* Complex vowel placement.

A Khmer typography module would push your renderer in a very different direction from Latin ASCII.

---

## 3. Myanmar language — another strong technical choice

Why it fits:

* Geographic proximity to Thailand.
* Beautiful circular script.
* Complex Unicode rendering issues.

Interesting challenges:

* Combining marks.
* Stacking.
* Character positioning.

A Myanmar module would test whether Fdf42 can move beyond simple character grids.

---

## 4. Zhuang language — interesting because of your Tai-Kadai interest

This one is especially relevant because you previously asked about Tai-Kadai languages.

Why it fits:

* Closely related to Thai linguistically.
* Has a rich history of writing systems:

  * traditional Zhuang characters,
  * modern Latin-based Zhuang alphabet.

Interesting Fdf42 angle:

* Compare how the same language can be represented by different scripts.
* Visualize script evolution.

---

## 5. Tibetan language — if Buddhist studies become important

Since you mentioned Pali and Buddhism:

Why it fits:

* Large philosophical and religious literature.
* Strong calligraphic tradition.

Interesting typography:

* Vertical structure.
* Complex stacks.
* Historical manuscripts.

It could connect your:

* language interest,
* Buddhism interest,
* visual art interest.

---

## 6. Māori language — a good preservation-oriented choice

Why:

* Strong revitalization movement.
* Relatively accessible learning resources.
* Important indigenous cultural value.

For Fdf42:

* Easier entry point than some endangered scripts.
* Lets you focus on cultural visualization rather than only technical script problems.

---

## 7. Cherokee language — interesting writing-system choice

Why:

* Has its own syllabary created by Sequoyah.
* A rare example of an indigenous script with a relatively recent creation history.

For typography:

* Shows how a writing system can be designed around the sound structure of a language.

---

One caution: endangered-language study is most meaningful when it goes beyond collecting "cool scripts." The best contribution is usually to engage with existing communities, respect how speakers want their language represented, and use technology in ways that support their goals (documentation, education, accessibility, archives, or creative expression).

For Fdf42 specifically, I think **Ainu, Zhuang, Khmer, and Myanmar** are especially aligned with your combination of Thai background, Japanese interest, and typography goals.
